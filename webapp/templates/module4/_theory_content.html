An edge is a place where intensity changes over a short distance. A short distance is a high spatial frequency, so an edge detector is a high-pass filter. Smoothing is the opposite: a low-pass filter removes those frequencies and the edges go soft.

Derivative theorem

Differentiate a function and the Fourier transform picks up a ramp in frequency. With \(u\) and \(v\) in cycles per pixel,

\[ \mathcal{F}\!\left\{\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right\} = j 2\pi u\, F(u,v), \qquad \mathcal{F}\!\left\{\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right\} = j 2\pi v\, F(u,v). \]

The gradient magnitude is \(\sqrt{|\partial f/\partial x|^2 + |\partial f/\partial y|^2}\). In the discrete image this is one FFT, two multiplies, and two inverse FFTs. No kernel is slid across the picture.

Laplacian

The Laplacian is the sum of the second derivatives. Differentiating twice multiplies by \((j 2\pi u)^2 = -4\pi^2 u^2\), so

\[ \mathcal{F}\{\nabla^2 f\} = -4\pi^2 (u^2 + v^2)\, F(u,v). \]

On a grid the matching operator is the neighbor sum \(L f = f_{x-1} + f_{x+1} + f_{y-1} + f_{y+1} - 4f\). Its DFT is exact, not an approximation:

\[ H(u,v) = -4 + 2\cos(2\pi u) + 2\cos(2\pi v). \]

The Taylor step \(\cos\theta \approx 1 - \theta^2/2\) turns \(H\) into \(-4\pi^2(u^2+v^2)\) when \(u\) and \(v\) are small. At high frequency the cosine saturates and the continuous formula does not. The code checks the discrete form: the neighbor sum and the multiply-by-\(H\) agree to roundoff (about \(10^{-15}\) on a random array). Edges of the Laplacian are its zero crossings, the same idea as the Marr–Hildreth detector.

High-pass shapes, and ringing

Let \(D(u,v)\) be the distance from DC (the center of the shifted spectrum) and \(D_0\) the cutoff.

  • Ideal: \(H = 0\) when \(D \le D_0\), and \(1\) otherwise. The cutoff is a sharp disk, whose inverse transform oscillates. Those oscillations show up as rings beside strong edges (the Gibbs effect).
  • Butterworth of order \(n\): \(H = 1 / (1 + (D_0/D)^{2n})\). Larger \(n\) is closer to the ideal disk, so it rings more.
  • Gaussian: \(H = 1 - \exp(-D^2 / (2 D_0^2))\). The transform of a Gaussian is a Gaussian, so this one does not ring. Its complement is the blur from Module 3.

A region map from the same filters is the low-pass image \( (1-H) F \), then Otsu. Smoothing suppresses edges; the threshold then splits the smooth picture into pieces.

LoG and DoG

The Laplacian of a Gaussian is a band-pass: the Gaussian kills the highest noise, and the Laplacian kills DC. A difference of Gaussians,

\[ \mathrm{DoG} = G_{\sigma_1} * f - G_{\sigma_2} * f, \qquad \sigma_2 > \sigma_1, \]

approximates it. Zero crossings of the DoG are the edges. The sign of the DoG is a coarse region label (bright blob versus dark surround).

Texture from local spectra

A Gabor filter is a sinusoid under a Gaussian envelope, so it responds to one orientation and one spacing. A bank of them (several angles, two widths, two wavelengths) turns texture into energy \(\sqrt{\sum r_i^2}\). Otsu on that energy separates textured regions from flat ones, which a plain intensity threshold cannot do when the two regions have the same average gray level.

Worked numbers

Take a row of \(N\) pixels and the frequency bin \(k = 1\), so \(u = 1/N\) cycles per pixel and \(v = 0\).

\[ H_{\text{discrete}} = -4 + 2\cos(2\pi / N) + 2, \qquad H_{\text{continuous}} = -4\pi^2 / N^2. \]

For \(N = 8\): \(\cos(2\pi/8) = \cos(\pi/4) = \sqrt{2}/2\), so \(H_{\text{discrete}} = -2 + \sqrt{2} \approx -0.5858\) and \(H_{\text{continuous}} = -\pi^2/16 \approx -0.6169\).
