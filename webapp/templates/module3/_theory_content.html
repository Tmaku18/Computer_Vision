What blur is

A blur replaces every pixel by a weighted average of the pixels around it. The weights are a small array \(h\), the kernel. With the kernel centered at index \((c_y, c_x)\),

\[ (f * h)[y, x] = \sum_{i,j} h[i, j]\; f[y + c_y - i,\; x + c_x - j]. \tag{1} \]

That sum is convolution. Sliding the kernel across the image without flipping it is correlation, which is what cv2.filter2D computes. The two agree for a symmetric kernel (a Gaussian, a box) and disagree for an asymmetric one (a diagonal motion streak). This code flips the kernel before calling filter2D, so the OpenCV result is a convolution too.

Convolution theorem, continuous

The Fourier transform turns a shift into a complex spin (a phase). Shifting \(f\) by \(a\) multiplies \(F(\omega)\) by \(e^{-j\omega a}\). Convolution is a weighted sum of every possible shift of \(f\), so each shift contributes its own phase and the weights factor out:

\[ \mathcal F\{f * h\}(\omega) = F(\omega)\, H(\omega). \tag{2} \]

In words: convolve in space, or multiply the transforms and transform back. Both give \(f * h\). A blur kernel is non-negative and sums to 1, so \(H(0) = 1\) (the average brightness is kept) and \(|H(\omega)|\) falls as \(|\omega|\) grows (fine detail, which lives at high frequency, is attenuated). That is why blur is called a low-pass filter. A Gaussian is special: its transform is another Gaussian, \(\mathcal F\{e^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)}\} \propto e^{-2\pi^2 \sigma^2 \xi^2}\), so a wider blur cuts high frequencies harder.

The discrete version, and why padding matters

The DFT assumes its input repeats forever. Multiplying DFTs therefore computes a circular convolution, where an index that falls off the left edge re-enters on the right:

\[ (f \circledast h)[n] = \sum_{k} h[k]\; f[(n - k) \bmod N], \qquad \mathrm{DFT}(f \circledast h) = \mathrm{DFT}(f)\cdot\mathrm{DFT}(h). \tag{3} \]

The DFT here is \(\displaystyle F[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} f[n] e^{-j 2\pi kn/N}\), and the inverse divides by \(N\). The kernel has to be placed so its center sits at index 0; otherwise the result is shifted.

A picture does not wrap around. The ordinary (linear) convolution treats everything outside the image as 0. That matches the DFT exactly once both arrays are zero-padded to length \(N = \mathrm{len}(f) + \mathrm{len}(h) - 1\) in each dimension, because the padded tails never reach around far enough to touch the real samples. Cropping the inverse DFT back to the original size, aligned on the kernel center, gives equation (1).

A reflected border — copying pixels back on themselves outside the frame — is a third rule. It looks the most natural, and it is still a blur, but it is not what the DFT does, so those two images differ in a strip along the border. The experiment table shows this on purpose: the reflected-border rows are the ones that do not match.

Worked example

Take \(f = [1, 2, 3, 4]\) and \(h = [1, 2, 1]\). Outside the four samples, \(f\) is 0. Equation (1) in one dimension:

\[ \begin{align*} n=0 &\colon 1\cdot 1 = 1\\ n=1 &\colon 2\cdot 1 + 1\cdot 2 = 4\\ n=2 &\colon 3\cdot 1 + 2\cdot 2 + 1\cdot 1 = 8\\ n=3 &\colon 4\cdot 1 + 3\cdot 2 + 2\cdot 1 = 12\\ n=4 &\colon 4\cdot 2 + 3\cdot 1 = 11\\ n=5 &\colon 4\cdot 1 = 4 \end{align*} \]

So \(f * h = [1, 4, 8, 12, 11, 4]\). Now pad both to length \(N = 4 + 3 - 1 = 6\) and multiply DFTs. The inverse DFT returns the same six numbers, with a gap of about \(10^{-15}\). The same-size blur, aligned on the center tap of \(h\), is the middle crop \([4, 8, 12, 11]\). The panel below recomputes every product from whatever signal and kernel you type.
